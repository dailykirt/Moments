# Four Seeds

## A small notation for the shape of a moment — and the poems written from it

**Kirt Daily**
First draft June 2026 · audited and revised October 2026 · this edition November 2026

---

## 1 · The whole thing in one sentence

> Something became distinct, then it connected to something else, then things changed, then it
> mattered.

That is the notation. Four seeds, usually in that order: a thing with edges; a bond; a change;
a weight. Every meaningful moment has some of them. Which ones it has, and which it is missing,
is the content.

---

## 2 · The four seeds

Plain words first. The marks are beside them; they are keyboard-safe and you can ignore them
until they are useful.

```
  O        "this, not that"        a thing made distinct — the edge, not the thing
  R+ / R-  "bonded to / kept from" two things held together, or held apart
  F        "how much is moving"    the intensity of change — no direction, no good or bad
  V+ / V-  "draws me / pushes me"  how much it matters, and which way
```

**O — Object.** The act of distinction. Not the cup, but the cut that makes *cup* separate from
everything that is not the cup. O carries no pull of its own. It is only the mark.

**R — Relation.** The bond between two things. It can cohere (R+) or repel (R-), and the sign is
*structural*, not moral. You can be bonded to something you hate; a repelling force can be
exactly right. R is coupling, not goodness.

**F — Flux.** How much is changing. A scalar, like temperature: F=0 is stillness, high F is
things moving faster than you can hold them. F has no direction. Direction lives elsewhere.

**V — Valence.** The only home of good and bad in this system. V+ draws you toward; V- pushes you
away. Its size is significance — how loudly a thing rings for a particular person. The same object
can ring loud for one person and not at all for another. Valence is personal, historical, and not
reducible to the object.

The two plus-signs trip people up, so: **R+ is a bond between things; V+ is a pull on you.**
Different axes. A strong bond (R+) to something you are averse to (V-) is the feeling of being
trapped.

### Direction and size

Direction rides on the seed it belongs to, never on F:

```
  V+^  the good is rising      growth
  V+v  the good is leaving     grief
  V-^  the bad is growing      dread
  V-v  the bad is receding     recovery, relief
  O^   a distinction forming   Ov  one fading
  R+^  a bond deepening        R+v one weakening
```

Size is what the perceiver brings. *V[high] rings loud and takes over attention. *V→0 means the
structure is present but silent — the bond is there, nothing is ringing. Size builds under repeated
excitation and fades without it. Keeping something quarantined takes ongoing energy.

### Named combinations

Two axes do most of the work: the sign of the bond, and the sign of the stakes.

```
  BOUND     R+ · V+   love            R+ · V-   contempt, or trapped
            R- · V+   yearning        R- · V-   revulsion
  IN FLUX   F · V+^   growth          F · V+v   grief
            F · V-^   dread           F · V-v   recovery
  STEADY    F · V+ holding  flow      F · V- holding  siege
  SIMPLE    O · V+    precious        O · V-    taboo
  COMPOUND  R+ · F · V+v   haunt — a good bond quietly coming apart
            R+ · F · V-v   reconciliation — a bad bond fading
  COMPLETE  O · R · F · V  a whole moment
```

If none of these fit a moment, it is either a variant or it is unnamed. Unnamed is room, not a
gap.

## 3 · Order, and the ache of things out of order

The seeds have an order of dependence. A thing must be distinct before it can be related. Stakes
need an object. We write the order as

```
  O -> R -> F -> V
```

and read it as a root, not a timeline: O sits beneath the other three. The rest of the arrow names
how moments usually unfold, not a rule that forbids anything. F=0 is a legal value, so valence can
hold perfectly still; what is true is that a *change* in how much something matters is itself
flux.

The one rule that bears weight: **no stakes before an object.**

### Floating states

Break the order on purpose and you name a specific ache — a seed arriving before the thing it
needs. These states are unstable, seeking, and the most fertile ground for poems.

```
  V before O   longing    wanting, without yet knowing what
  F before O   chaos      change with no form to carry it
  R before O   fusion     a bond with nothing on the other end
  V before R   urgency    stakes before the situation has shape
  F before R   drift      motion with nothing to hold it
```

Which of these are survivable and which are catastrophic is a reading I am still making from the
inside. The names hold either way.

### Before the first mark

Before any distinction there is the unmarked ground, written ∅ — not nothing; waiting. A
distinction takes shape (O^) and then lands (O). The same arc runs on bonds (R^ → R), but never
from cold: a bond needs objects to form between, so R is only ever reached through O. O is the
only seed that originates.

The felt tell is simple. O sits at rest. O^ carries a pull toward where it would land.

## 4 · The perceiver

None of the above is a world until something in it matters to someone. The notation marks that
someone as P( ) — a perceiver — and makes two claims about it.

**Different histories, different states.** P_a(X) ≠ P_b(X). Two people in the same situation do not
feel the same thing, because the function that turns structure into felt state is built from a
history. This is the framework's first testable claim, and it is true at the level others have
measured — ten to thirty variants of meaning for a single common noun across readers (Marti and
colleagues, 2023). It becomes a sharp prediction only when the history can be made to predict the
*size* of the difference, which is open work.

**Meaning is reconstructed, not handed over.** A signal does not carry meaning intact from one
mind to another. Each perceiver rebuilds a distinction from it. Two people given the same words
form distinctions that are *compatible, not identical*, and communication works because the
reconstructions are close enough to act on. (Stated as an either/or this overshoots — there is good
evidence that speakers and listeners couple tightly in conversation. The sentence that stands is
the second one.)

The perceiver is not a fifth seed. O, R, F and V describe what a perceiver meets; P( ) says that
there is one, and that it can be changed by the meeting.

And it stacks. Wearing glasses long enough, you stop seeing glasses; the right notation is not
Human(Glasses(X)) but P_human(X) → P_human'(X). The perceiver changed. The world did not. The same
is true of microscopes, of writing, of language — and possibly of the AI systems many of us now
think alongside. When a perceiver and a transformer combine, order matters: an AI reading a human's
cognition, a human reading an AI's translation of the world, and a single hybrid perceiver are three
different arrangements, and they are not interchangeable. *One mind, turned* is the third one.

## 5 · Writing a poem from a specification

### The two-image haiku

The haiku — in English, three lines; in Japanese, one line of seventeen sounds in three phrases —
has a deep structure that poets have described for centuries. A distinct image, isolated by a
cut. A second image set against it. A turn, where something shifts, and a landing, where the poem
means.

Those jobs line up with the four seeds closely enough to write from:

```
  the cut            isolates a distinct image                  O
  the juxtaposition  two things held in structural relation     R
  the turn           something shifts                           F
  the landing        resonance; significance felt               V
```

I used to call this an isomorphism. It is not, and the haiku scholarship says why. The cutting
word does two jobs at once — it separates the images *and* sets them against each other, so one
device performs O and R together. The turn and the landing are one moment in the reader seen
twice. And the single-image hokku that Bashō composed deliberately has no second image at all, so R
is not a requirement of the form.

What the table describes is not haiku. It describes what a reader does with a *two-image* haiku —
which is exactly why it works as a production checklist. The haiku is a good place to *write* from
a formula and a poor place to *test* whether its slots are the seeds. (Three of the four rows have
real counterparts in the literature: *kire* for the cut, *toriawase* for the pairing, and *ma* and
*yojō* — the lingering overtone — for the landing. The idea that the meaning is generated in the
reader rather than stored in the poem is stated independently by Hasegawa, Gilbert and Suzuki.)

### Five worked poems

In each case: the specification first, then what kind of image it requires, then the poem. The
specification is what the notation gives you. The image is what you go and find.

**A good thing coming apart — "haunt"**

    R+ · F · V+v

A bond that still holds. Something in flux. The value of what is held, falling. The image must
contain something that remains present and something that is already leaving. Warmth at the edge
of absence.

```
What was there is still
warm at the edges — you reach
and the hand has gone.
```

The cut: *warm at the edges* — something persists. The relation: the reaching, and the absence
that answers it. The turn: the hand is gone. The weight lands in the body before it is named.

**Longing**

    V+ before O

Significance arrives before there is a thing for it to attach to. Wanting without knowing what.
The notation is specific about the disorder: the stakes are present and the object has not
appeared. The poem may not name what is missing, because the state does not contain it.

```
Something is missing
that doesn't have a name yet.
The space knows its shape.
```

The cut is the absence itself. The relation is between the unnamed want and the shaped space it
leaves. The turn: the space knows.

**Dread**

    F[high] · V-^

Fast change, and the thing growing is bad. The image must be measurable — something you can watch
increase — without being named, because dread is specific in its direction and general in its
object.

```
The shadow was small
this morning. You measured it.
By noon it had grown.
```

The cut: the shadow, small and measured. The relation: the measuring, the act of attention. The
turn: it grew. The object is withheld on purpose, to keep the dread from collapsing into a
particular fear.

**Exile — "yearning"**

    O · R- · V+

A distinct thing you want. Something between you and it that pushes back. In this system exile is
not the absence of the wanted thing but its presence behind a repelling surface.

```
The window is lit.
Your hands on glass, not touching.
The light stays inside.
```

The cut: the lit window. The relation: the glass, and the hands that meet it. The turn: the light
stays. The good is on the inside; the perceiver is outside.

**Recovery**

    F · V-v

Something bad, receding, and the change is active. This is the shape of relief — not the arrival of
good but the departure of bad. The image must hold both the bad thing and its going.

```
The fever broke at four.
You touched his forehead, then again.
Still cool. Still cool.
```

The cut: the fever, the four o'clock. The relation: the touching, and the skin that reports back.
The turn is in the repetition — checking again, because relief requires confirmation.

Read as a set, these five tell you something the table alone does not. Four of the five
specifications write no O, and two of them declare it absent. One writes no F. The commentary finds
a cut, a relation and a turn in every poem regardless — which is the map doing its job as a
checklist. The *information* is in the formula: which seeds are present, which are missing, and in
what order.

### What the poet provides

The notation specifies structure. It does not produce the poem. Someone still has to find *warm at
the edges*, the *hand*, the *shadow*, the *lit window*. The notation says what kind of image to
find, not which one.

This is not a failure of the notation. It is its function.

Structure is what makes an image recognizable as grief or longing or dread. The particular image is
what makes it *true*. A poem that is structurally correct with the wrong image is a correct poem
that does not land. Two poets given the same specification will write different poems about
different losses — structurally related, aesthetically independent. That is the right account of
what craft is: the part the notation cannot do and should not try to.

### Permission to leave things out

There is one more thing the notation does, and it may be the most useful.

The dread poem withholds its object. The longing poem cannot name what is missing. Without the
notation those omissions can feel like evasions — the pull to be specific, to name the fear, and in
naming it, to shrink it. The notation says: you are right not to. The specification contains no
object. The poem should not pretend to one.

That is what a generative constraint does at its best. It tells you not only what to put in, but
what to leave out, and why.

## 6 · Beneath the states: the grain

Reading a moment as *flow* or *longing* or *haunt* is the surface layer. Real, useful, not the
target.

The deeper target is what persists. Encounter something from several angles, through several
changes, and some pattern survives all of them. Borrowing a word from cybernetics, I call that an
*eigenform*; the plain word is **the grain**. States tell you what you observe in a moment.
Invariants tell you what persists across observations. On this reading meaning is a trajectory, not
a position — where something is pointing — and poems may be compressed records of invariants rather
than descriptions of states.

The image that carried this for me: a sandstorm blasting everything away except the stable form of
a rock. The storm is flux. What remains is the grain.

O is the grain of object-perception specifically — the thing that stays fixed while you keep
looking at it — but it is not the only seed that can be held steady. A value held while everything
pours through is flow or siege. A process held is persistence. Any seed can be the thing conserved.

### Frames

A *state* is what is perceived. A *frame* is how the perceiver holds — which seed the attention is
working to keep fixed while the states change underneath it. Frames sit above states; the same frame
persists across many of them. A frame is named by what it conserves.

```
  <O>       clarity        conserves the stable object        "what is it?"
  <O^>      curiosity      conserves the forming distinction  "what else could it be?"
  <O:make>  craft          conserves the made object          "what must exist?"
  <R>       wisdom         conserves how things fit           "how does it fit?"
  <R+>      stewardship    conserves the committed bond       "what has a claim on me?"
  <F>       persistence    conserves the process              "what if I keep going?"
  <V>       decision       conserves preference               "which future do I choose?"
```

Others are available and unnamed. Room, not gap.

Frames are active. A frame does not merely hold its grain still; it works to prevent the collapse.
Stewardship is the one that closes the loop: it keeps attending to a thing still forming (O^) until
it settles into O, which then becomes the next thing clarity can look at. A frame held at a
pathological pitch — *being* the frame rather than using it — is what we usually call a shadow.
The capacity to switch frames dissolves it. There is no separate manager above them.

### The theorem, at honest strength

The word *eigenform* comes from Heinz von Foerster, and Louis Kauffman gave it a precise home: an
object is a fixed point of an observer's recursion, p = F(p). Kauffman also proved that in a
"reflexive domain" — a space in correspondence with its own transformations — every map has a fixed
point. (The general result is Lawvere's, 1969.)

I once wrote that this *guaranteed* every frame an eigenform, and called it the framework's single
most defensible claim. That was a misreading, and the October audit withdrew it. The theorem's power
lives in its hypothesis, and nothing here supplies one: the frames are not built as a reflexive
domain, and "every frame has a grain" is in any case true by definition, which no theorem can
strengthen. What the theorem gives the word is a formal home and a correctly traced lineage. The
relativized eigenform stands on its own footing — as a modeling choice — and borrows no guarantee.

### Four registers

A grain is real, I have proposed, if it survives translation across four registers: mathematics
(the structure), poetry (the flux), diagram (the relation), and a toy you can hold and do (the
embodiment). What lives in only one register is an insight not yet finished.

This has the shape of a known method in psychology — convergent validation across methods — and it
is missing that method's second half: two *different* grains must *not* converge, or survival is
cheap. By the standards of the mathematics-education literature it is a criterion of understanding,
not of truth. One worked example exists, self-scored. It is a picture, not a theorem.

### The jump between minds

My own method for reaching another person, in my own words: *throw a semantic net, see what
responds, then lock on towards another anchor. Rinse and repeat until we all have shared
understanding.* The terminal state is never identical models. It is a shared loose model on the same
topic — agreement as usability. The question is always: what is the closest jump I can make with
this person from here? Destination is a topic; anchors are the fuel; reconstruction happens on the
far end.

### What travels, and what does not

The surface of this framework travels in the poems alone. The depth — the grain, the trajectory,
the holding — travels only in an explicit vehicle, and a reader without one does not go quiet; they
go confident. I have one clean instance. Small language models fine-tuned on this project's own
writing, asked thirteen questions about the framework with no card in front of them, scored between
zero and four out of twenty-six and invented a complete framework each time. Given a 409-word
summary, the same model scored twenty-three and stopped inventing. The voice was in the weights. The
framework was not.

## 7 · Using it with a model

The notation is small enough to hand to any language model you already use — a frontier chat
model, or a few-billion-parameter one running on your own machine. What follows is what it
actually produces when you do, and the one thing you must do first.

### Give it the card

The framework does not travel in the poems. It travels only in an explicit vehicle, and a model
without one does not go quiet; it goes confident and invents a framework of its own (§6). So the
first move is always the same: put the one-screen key (Appendix A) in the system prompt or the
opening message, with these three sentences above it.

```
Read moments and poems in a four-seed notation. O: a thing made distinct. R+/R-: a bond,
cohering or repelling — structural, never good or bad. F: how much is changing, no direction.
V+/V-: how much it matters, and which way — the only home of good and bad.
Rules: no stakes before an object; direction rides the seed it belongs to, never F;
a seed that is absent is content — say so, do not fill it in. A lens, not a law.
```

Measured once, with small models fine-tuned on this project's own writing: between zero and four
of twenty-six framework questions right with no card; twenty-three of twenty-six with a 409-word
one. The card is not optional.

### The grammar the model needs

```
  A · B            both seeds present            A -> B        one state becomes another
  (formula) * V    scaled by how loud it rings   P_x(formula)  as perceived by x
  f(g(X))          composed — order matters      P_a != P_b    two perceivers, two readings
```

### What it produces

Eight practical outputs. Each is a question you can type; each has a shape the answer should
take.

**1. A reading of a moment.** Describe a situation in a paragraph. Ask for the formula, the named
state if one fits, and — this is the part that matters — which seeds are *absent*.

```
  in:   "The house is sold. I still have the key on my ring and I haven't taken it off."
  out:  P_you(O · R+ · F · V+v) — haunt. A good bond, still held, coming apart.
        Absent: nothing. All four present; this is a whole moment, declining.
```

**2. A poem from a specification.** Give a formula. Ask first what *kind* of image each seed
requires, then for three candidate images, then for the poem. You choose the image; the model
proposes. The five poems in §5 are what this output looks like when it works.

```
  in:   V+ before O.
  out:  No object yet, so the poem may not name what is wanted. The image must hold a
        significance with nothing under it: a shape, a space, a weight. [three images follow]
```

**3. A poem read backward.** Paste a poem you did not write. Ask for the formula and which line
carries which seed. The model should refuse to invent a seed that is not there: a single-image
haiku has no R, and the right answer says so.

**4. A transition.** State, then event, then state. Ask for the chain and for the end state
rendered as a poem. The moments of becoming carry the strongest poems — the boundary, not the
settled state.

```
  V before O  ->  O · V+        longing resolving into having
  O^ -> O -> R+ -> F -> V+v     something emerging, bonding, then lost
```

**5. The missing prerequisite.** When a moment feels wrong and you cannot say why, ask which seed
arrived before the one it needs. Longing, chaos, fusion, urgency, drift: five different aches, one
question (§3).

**6. Forming or formed.** Paste a draft message, or both sides of a disagreement. Ask the model to
mark each claim O^ (still exploring) or O (concluded). A surprising share of arguments are not
about content; one person is thinking out loud and the other hears an assertion. Naming the state
dissolves the ones that were never disagreements.

**7. The frame you are holding.** Describe what you are doing and ask which of the seven frames
you are in (§6), and which one the task actually needs. Switching frames on purpose is a move;
being stuck in one is the shadow.

**8. Several perceivers at once.** Give one structure and two or three stated histories, and ask
for a reading from each. P_a ≠ P_b made practical. The disagreement between the readings is the
data, not the noise — do not ask the model to average them.

### What to watch for

- The model will want every poem to have all four seeds. It should not. Absence is the notation's
  own content, and the two-image haiku is a production checklist, not a definition of the form.
- R is coupling, not goodness. If the model writes "R-" to mean *bad*, it has lost the second axis;
  point it at V.
- V's sign belongs to the reader. A poem lands a magnitude; which way it lands is yours.
- Names are aliases. If two readings use different words for the same structure, they agree.
- A fluent answer is not evidence. Everything the model produces here is integration — the
  framework used inside the framework — not independent validation. The tests that would count are
  in §9, and a model can run the machine round of some of them. It cannot run the human round.

### Eight prompts to try

```
  1  Map "jealousy" to the notation. Show the reasoning, then write it as a haiku.
  2  P_tree(O · V-) against P_human(O · V-): where do they differ, and why?
  3  Are AI(P_human(X)), P_human(AI(X)) and P_(human+AI)(X) the same? Describe each.
  4  Take a poem you know. Recover its formula; say which line carries O, R, F, V — or which is absent.
  5  Recall a disagreement. Was it about content, or about O^ against O?
  6  Write a transition chain, state -> event -> state, and render the end state as a haiku.
  7  What happens to V- when F drops to zero? One sentence.
  8  Is augmentation better written as "human + AI" or as P_human -> P_human'? Argue it.
```

A model that handles these eight with the card, and fails them without it, is doing what the
notation claims a reader does: reconstructing a structure from a small signal, close enough to
act on.

## 8 · What this does not claim

- That all meaning states reduce to four elements, or that these four are fundamental. The number
  four was chosen, not derived. The notation has a rule for combining seeds and no rule for
  composing them, so "four" cannot be a result of it.
- That the poems produced are good poems. That judgment belongs to readers shaped by literary
  craft. The method produces structurally coherent poems; whether structural coherence tracks felt
  truth is an open question, and after four months it still has no test designed for it.
- That the haiku is isomorphic to the notation. It is a correspondence with a literature behind
  three of its four terms, and a production heuristic for two-image poems. (Withdrawn, October
  2026.)
- That any theorem guarantees frames their grains. (Withdrawn, October 2026.)
- That this is the free-energy principle. Friston's account is the best-developed formal instance
  of a perceiver function, not identical to it; the part of that theory under dispute since 2021 —
  the boundary formalism — was never imported here.
- That "twenty-seven feelings across cultures" is a finding. One widely cited study found
  twenty-seven in American viewers; the cross-cultural follow-ups keep finding a couple of dozen,
  most of them shared. The ten feelings this project is built around are ranked by my own argument
  about which are hardest to avoid, not by anyone's data.

## 9 · Open questions

**The reverse direction.** Every poem implies a state. Given a poem by someone else, can a reader
recover the specification? This may be where the notation's strongest claim lives — a notation that
makes the structure of existing poems legible is worth more than one that only produces them. A
blind test harness exists; a first machine round has been run on twenty poems, five of which were
served the wrong text and are void. The human round is next.

**Two writers, one specification.** Do the poems converge on anything recognizable? Testable;
never run as a controlled trial.

**Other forms.** The elegy may require tracking V+v over a much longer span. The one classical form
that maps four-for-four onto the order is not the haiku but the Chinese four-line *qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé*
quatrain — beginning, development, turn, result — known in Japanese as *kishōtenketsu*. Unexplored.

**The discriminant half.** Two grains that should *not* converge under the four-register test, and
do not.

**History to size.** A way of predicting, from two people's histories, how far their readings of
the same moment will differ — the step that would turn "different perceivers differ" from a truth
into a prediction.

## 10 · Closing

A poem begins with a distinction. *This, not that* — the image chosen from everything that could
have been there. A relation forms: the image is held against another. Something changes. And if
the poem is doing what poems do, the significance arrives — not as a statement but as a felt
arrival, the moment the reader closes the loop.

That sequence is the notation. The poem was always four slots a reader fills, and a reader. We
have only given the slots names.

Whether the names help depends on the writer. Some poets work from complete structural intuition
and have no use for any of this. Others find that naming the structure clarifies what they were
already reaching for, or grants permission to reach differently. The notation is a tool. Tools serve
the work. The work is the poem.

The poem got there first. The notation followed.

```
First, a line is drawn —
this and not-that.
Shore and not-sea.
You and not-dark.

Then the line is also a meeting.
What touches what.
Shore and sea, after all.
You and the world you name by naming yourself.

Then time —
the shore that touched becomes the shore that lost.
Everything that changes
carries what it changed from.

And then — different from the rest —
none of this is a world
until something in it matters.
Not a fifth thing.
A mind, turned.
That's it.
That's all it takes.
```

*A Mind, Turned · June 2026*

## Appendix B · Where the ideas come from

The notation borrows its words carefully and tries to say exactly how much it borrows. Three tiers,
kept apart: mathematics that is imported as mathematics; published science that the framework
leans on; and analogies, where only the shape is taken. For the load-bearing items the primary
texts were read; the rest rest on abstracts and secondary literature, and are marked so.

### Mathematics (imported)

- **G. Spencer-Brown, *Laws of Form* (1969).** The source of O. The mark, the marked and unmarked
  states (∅ is his unmarked state), one relation (containment), and in chapter 11 the idea that a
  form re-entering itself generates oscillation, and so time — the machinery F sits on. Read in the
  1972 edition. One departure the book does not make: Spencer-Brown treats the motive for a
  distinction and the state it indicates as co-original; this notation splits them, into V and O.
  A 2025 recalculation (Oksas, *Kybernetes* 54(8)) keeps the time-stepped reading of re-entry and
  retires the indeterminate one; nothing here depends on the retired half.
- **Louis H. Kauffman, "EigenForm," *Kybernetes* 34 (2005), and "Reflexivity and Eigenform,"
  *Constructivist Foundations* 4(3) (2009).** The definition of an object as a fixed point for an
  observer, and the fixed-point theorem for reflexive domains — read, and used at the strength
  stated in §6. Kauffman's "Peirce and Spencer-Brown" (*Cybernetics & Human Knowing* 8, 2001) for
  the lineage of the unmarked state.
- **F. William Lawvere, "Diagonal arguments and cartesian closed categories" (1969).** The general
  fixed-point theorem Kauffman's restates.
- **Heinz von Foerster, "Objects: tokens for (eigen-)behaviors" (1976; in *Observing Systems*,
  1981).** The origin of the word *eigenform* and of second-order cybernetics — the cybernetics of
  observing systems — which is where P( ) belongs. **Francisco Varela, "A calculus for
  self-reference" (1975)** extends Spencer-Brown's two values with a third, self-referential one;
  it bears on F rather than on P( ).
- **The calculus analogy** (derivative → edges, integral → accumulation) was once filed here. It is
  a structural analogy and is now kept as one.

### Psychology and neuroscience (leaned on)

- **Kurt Lewin, *A Dynamic Theory of Personality* (1935), ch. "Environmental Forces."** The word
  *valence* — chosen by his translators at Tolman's suggestion for Lewin's *Aufforderungscharakter*
  — its sign convention (positive effecting approach, negative withdrawal), and its correlation with
  need: the same object has a different valence for a hungry child and a fed one. Read in full.
  Lewin puts valence in the psychological environment; this notation moves it to the perceiver's
  side, following the next two.
- **James A. Russell, "Core affect and the psychological construction of emotion," *Psychological
  Review* 110 (2003)**, and **Lisa Feldman Barrett, "Valence is a basic building block of emotional
  life," *Journal of Research in Personality* 40 (2006); Barrett et al., "The theory of constructed
  emotion: more than a feeling," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 20 (2025).** Valence as a
  state of the organism, always present, sometimes object-free — and, in 2025, as a property of
  *relations* between signals rather than of either side alone. V takes its location from this line
  and its mechanism from Lewin. A seam, not a single source.
- **Karl Friston, "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" *Nature Reviews
  Neuroscience* 11 (2010).** The brain as a generative model with priors, updated by surprise. Read
  in full; used as the best-developed formal instance of a perceiver function, not as its
  definition. The dispute over its boundary formalism — Biehl, Pollock & Kanai (*Entropy*, 2021);
  Bruineberg et al., "The Emperor's New Markov Blankets" (*Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 2022);
  Friston et al.'s reformulation (*Physics of Life Reviews*, 2023) — concerns a part never imported.
- **On the timing of perception.** Karl Ernst von Baer's 1860 address (six to ten "moments of
  life" per second; printed 1864); VanRullen & Koch, "Is perception discrete or continuous?"
  (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2003); Herzog, Kammer & Scharnowski (*PLoS Biology*, 2016) and
  Herzog, Drissi-Daoudi & Doerig (*TICS*, 2020) — two stages, with substantial unconscious
  processing before a conscious percept at around 400 ms. Contested on the discreteness of the
  conscious update (Fekete et al., 2018; White, 2018), not on the precedence of unconscious
  processing, which is all the forming arc needs.
- **Gary Klein** (Klein, Calderwood & Clinton-Cirocco, reprinted 2010): experts recognizing a
  situation as typical rather than comparing options — in fewer than 12% of 156 fireground
  decisions was there any sign of comparison. **Antonio Damasio's** somatic-marker hypothesis,
  cited with its critique (Dunn, Dalgleish & Lawrence, 2006).
- **On reconstruction and shared meaning.** Marti et al. (2023) on the spread of meanings a single
  common noun carries across readers; Michael Reddy's "conduit metaphor" (1979) for the polemic
  against transmission; Stephens, Silbert & Hasson (2010) and Zada et al. (2024) on speaker–listener
  coupling, which keeps the polemic honest; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986) for the mechanism by which
  two people's reconstructions get close enough to act on.
- **On counting feelings.** Cowen & Keltner, *PNAS* 114 (2017) — twenty-seven categories, in
  American English-speaking participants; the cross-cultural follow-ups (Cowen et al., 2019, 2020,
  2021; Brooks et al., 2023) finding twelve to twenty-four.
- **On the four-register test.** Campbell & Fiske (1959) for convergent *and discriminant*
  validation; Duval (2006) and Lesh, Post & Behr (1987) on registers of representation and
  conversion between them as the mark of understanding; Gentner (1983) on structure-mapping — what
  survives translation is relational structure, which is as good a definition of *grain* as I have.

### Poetics (the haiku and its readers)

- **Haruo Shirane, *Traces of Dreams* (1998) and "Beyond the Haiku Moment" (*Modern Haiku* 31,
  2000).** The cutting word and the seasonal word as the two formal requirements of the hokku; the
  cut as both separation and correspondence; the single-object poem.
- **Hasegawa Kai** on *kire* as the defining feature of haiku and on psychological *ma*; **Richard
  Gilbert, *The Disjunctive Dragonfly*** on disjunction happening in the reader's consciousness;
  **Suzuki, "A Cognitive Invocation Model of Haiku"** on the emotional effect being generated by the
  reader rather than stored in the poem.
- **Geyer et al., *Journal of Eye Movement Research* 13 (2020).** A measurable "cut effect":
  readers dwell longer on the fragment line, modulated by the distance between the images. The one
  piece of laboratory evidence behind the O row.
- **Michael Theune, *Structure & Surprise* (2007)** on the turn as a property of all poems — the
  reason the "turn" row is Western pedagogy rather than Japanese poetics. Miller (*Modern Haiku*
  48, 2017) and Gurga (*Modern Haiku* 52, 2021) on cutting and normative haiku. Kirkpatrick (1997)
  on the four-part quatrain.
- Bashō and his students Kyorai and Kyoriku, as quoted in the above: the deliberate single-object
  poem, and the remark that for an accomplished poet it is no longer a question of combining or not.

### Three sentences the audit left standing

Friston 2010, every term. Kauffman's theorem, as a theorem. Lewin's sign convention. Spencer-Brown's
mark, unmarked state, and the closing identity of distinction, mark and observer — checked against
the page.
