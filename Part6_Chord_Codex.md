## 1 · The whole thing in one sentence

> A moment can be read as a chord: four marks, each lit or dark. Sixteen chords cover every combination,
> and what the chord is *for* is never one of the marks. The reader brings that.

Part 1 gave four seeds — a thing made distinct (`O`), a bond (`R`: `R+` when it holds, `R-` when it
repels), a change (`F`) and a weight (`V`) — and the states they make. Part 5 looked for what stays
underneath them. This part is a tool for analyzing a single moment: four questions of its own, sixteen
chords that cover every way of answering them, and a short poem for each chord. You hold the four marks
up to a moment you have lived and see which forces were at work in it, including some you did not notice
from inside.

The chords come from a small book, *A Codex of Chords* — the codex, below — written first as a spellbook
for the imagination. That is why its chord names sound like spells, and why some of its poems speak of
casting. A spell runs forward, from the chord to what it does; read backward, from a moment already lived
to the chord struck in it, the same book analyzes moments. As it puts it: *a chord is what was struck in
a moment — this book reads it back. you do not cast; you DECODE.*

The four marks are close kin to Part 1's four seeds, but they are not the same four; §2 says how they
differ. §3 gives the method, §4 the sixteen chords, and §5 works through five moments with them; Appendix
A puts the whole tool on one screen.

**A lens, not a law** — and here a lighter one than usual. The codex was written as fiction, and nothing
in it is offered as a new claim about the notation. §11 says how strong it is.

## 2 · The four marks

```
  mark      lit   dark   the codex calls it   what it marks in a moment
  object     ■     □     object               a thing that stays while the rest moves
  cut        ▦     □     filter               an edge drawn: this, not that
  change     ▲     △     change               something moving
  bond       ━     ─     relation             a tie that holds two things together
```

A chord is written as four marks, always in that order: object, cut, change, bond. A filled or heavy mark
is lit — *struck*, in the codex's own word; a hollow or light one is dark. To read a chord, find the
filled marks: `■ □ ▲ ─` is an object and a change, with no cut and no bond. Object and cut are drawn dark
the same way, `□`, so only the position tells them apart, which is why the order never changes. The order
is not Part 1's order of dependence, `O → R → F → V`: the codex puts change before the bond, and its
early key reads the four left to right as *concrete → abstract* — a thing you could point at, an edge, a
change, and a tie you cannot see.

The README, Part 1 and Part 4 call the seeds' letters *marks* too; in this part a mark is always one of
these four. The book calls them *dials*, and calls the second one the *filter*. I call it the *cut*,
after the codex's own gloss: *filter is the cut itself — "this, not that"*.

How the marks sit on the seeds is itself a reading, added in October and not yet adopted (§11). Here it
is.

**Object and cut are both `O`.** Part 1 §2 defines `O` as the act of distinction — not the cup, but the
cut that makes *cup* separate from everything that is not the cup. Part 5 §2 reads it from the other
side: the object is what survives every look. The private archive of working notes this series is drawn
from first called the seed `D`, for distinction, the act, and later renamed it `O`, for the object the
act produces: the same seed, named for its product. The codex keeps both as marks. The object is *the
thing that stays while the rest moves*; the cut is the act that makes it — in the codex's words, *the act
the thing is the product of*. So a chord asks a question the seeds leave open: in this moment, is the
object being *held*, being *cut*, or both?

**Change is `F`** — how much is moving, with no direction of its own. Whether a change makes or unmakes
is not in the mark.

**The bond is `R+` only.** The codex has a mark for a tie that holds and none for a tie that repels; it
leaves pulling-apart out by design. Where keeping something out does happen inside the codex, as in WARD
(§4), it is done by the cut, not by the bond. (Part 4 §4 is sparing with `R-` too: it names refusing,
*the No*, as an act someone does, not a stance someone holds.)

**Weight is not a mark.** `V` — whether it matters, and which way — is never written in a chord. Its
*which way* becomes the chord's *aim*: in the codex's words, every spell *asks one thing the dials
cannot: toward what?* The aim carries a second thing too, the *direction* the lit marks run — making or
unmaking: a distinction forming or fading, a bond deepening or loosening. Part 1 lets `O`, `R` and `V`
each carry their own direction — `^` rising or forming, `v` falling or fading, as in `O^` or `V+v` — and
never `F`; the codex hands all of it to the aim. A poem may be written running one way (§4); for the
moment you decode, which way it runs is still the aim's. How *much* a moment matters, how loudly it
rings, rides in the coat — everything the chord wears (§9). In a moment you analyze, the aim is yours to
supply. It has been off the marks since the book's first drafts, one of which says of `V`: *It does not
get a dial; it is the hand.* This is the codex keeping, in its own form, the rule the whole series runs
on: the structure is shared, and the weight is what the perceiver brings (Part 1 §2 and §4). In Part 3,
*the eigenform, orbited* says it in one line: *I carry no value -- the value is what you brought.*

## 3 · How to analyze a moment

This is the tool itself. You take a moment that already happened and hold the four marks up to it, which
the codex calls decoding.

1. **Take one beat, and do not ask what it meant yet.** One scene, small enough to hold whole. Meaning
   belongs to the aim, and the aim comes last.
2. **Ask the four questions, one per mark, in order: object, cut, change, bond.** Does a thing hold while
   the rest moves — or come to stay, or slip away? Was a line drawn, or erased: this, not that? Did
   something change? Is a tie at work — holding, forming, or loosening? A mark is lit when its force is
   at work in the moment, in either direction; which direction is the aim's to say.
3. **Write the marks, and find the chord.** Write them in that order, lit or dark as §2 draws them, and
   look the row up in §4 or in Appendix A's table. The marks can name forces you did not notice while you
   were inside the moment. The codex says it names them *so a foreign idea with no meaning gets tied to
   one that's yours*: a bare word like *cut* or *bond*, tied to a moment of your own.
4. **Then supply the aim.** Toward what? Making or unmaking? Good or bad? That part is yours.
5. **If you want, write the words.** A part for each lit mark, nothing for a dark one, and leave what it
   comes to mean to whoever reads it (§6).

Three working rules settle the doubts the moments in §5 run into. They are mine, drawn from the October
reading of the codex's own poems, and they cite those poems by chord name; each chord, with its marks and
its poem, is in §4.

- **Light the object only for a thing kept — one that stays, or one slipping away.** A thing the moment
  brings into being to stay counts too, as GENESIS's *a thing, cut from nothing* does. A thing picked out
  to be undone, like what BOLT burns or BANISH sends, is the cut's work and the change's, not the
  object's. A thing merely present, or merely at the far end of a tie, does not light it; nor does one
  already gone.
- **Light change only when something in the moment changes.** Motion around a thing that holds is its
  ground, not a lit change: ANCHOR's *the rest moves* belongs to the object. What the held thing does to
  what it holds is a change, as SPHERE's *all within, undone* is, and so is a tie's own loosening or
  mending, as in the moments of §5. A tie that only changes hands, as in COMPEL's *it turns to me*, is
  the bond re-pointed, not a lit change.
- **A barrier is a line drawn, so it lights the cut**, as in WARD; so does one thing picked out from
  others, as BOLT picks what burns. A push between two things is a repulsion and has no mark. Wanting is
  the aim, not a tie: *R+ is a bond between things; V+ is a pull on you* (Part 1 §2). Deciding is the
  aim's too (Part 4 §4): every act is decided, and a decision alone draws no line.

An early draft of the codex gave this kind of reading one rule, and it still holds: *render a thing
through a lens, look, then go live it. a snapshot — never a control panel for your insides.* And a line
later: *glance, name, set it down.* Elsewhere the book says it from the other side: *you can put the book
down.*

What comes out is a chord and an aim. The chord is structure two readers can compare; the aim is yours.
The error to watch for is lighting the bond because the story wants one (§9). §4 is the table to look the
chord up in, and §5 works through five moments with the method.

## 4 · The sixteen chords

Four marks, each lit or dark, make sixteen chords: two to the fourth power. That number is arithmetic,
not a discovery. Any four switches give sixteen patterns, so the table is complete and closed by
construction. Part 1 §8 makes the matching point about the seeds: four was chosen, not derived. What is
not arithmetic is whether these are the right four marks, and whether each name and poem fits its chord.

Grouped by how many marks are lit, the sixteen make a shape — narrow, wide, narrow. It is the
1 · 4 · 6 · 4 · 1 row of Pascal's triangle (Appendix B).

```
  none lit    1    VOID                                                     the hand closed
  one lit     4    ANCHOR · DISCERN · TRANSMUTE · SENDING                   the single strikes
  two lit     6    WARD · PETRIFY · BULWARK · BOLT · COMPEL · MEND          the working body
  three lit   4    SPHERE · OATH · REFORGE · BANISH                         the heavy workings
  four lit    1    GENESIS                                                  the whole hand
```

Each chord below carries its marks, its name, its poem and a plain reading of the moment it names. The
names and poems are the book's own, unrevised. The plain readings are mine.

One thing before the table: the poems are written with a direction already in them — REFORGE gathers,
BANISH loosens — and my plain readings follow the poems, though only the void's poem says what its moment
comes to mean. The chord itself carries no direction. Under the opposite aim the same marks read the
other way: PETRIFY's change that sets becomes something set that starts to move, and REFORGE's repair
becomes an undoing. GENESIS is the one poem that says so out loud, and MEND's *whole, or gone* comes
close.

### One mark — the single strikes

```
  ANCHOR            ■ □ △ ─

       It stays.
       The rest moves.
```

*In a moment:* one thing holds while everything around it moves. The book's own example is a coat button
on a shelf, once it is set down — *a thing that will still be here tomorrow.*

```
  DISCERN           □ ▦ △ ─

       This. Not that.
```

*In a moment:* a line is drawn — a distinction made and declared.

```
  TRANSMUTE         □ □ ▲ ─

       What was —
       is not.
```

*In a moment:* something changes, and no marked thing carries the change.

```
  SENDING           □ □ △ ━

       Here —
       and there, held.
```

*In a moment:* a tie across a distance, with no thing marked at either end.

### Two marks — the working body

```
  WARD              ■ ▦ △ ─

       A line is drawn.
       What may not pass, does not.
```

*In a moment:* a boundary drawn around something kept.

```
  PETRIFY           ■ □ ▲ ─

       It was changing.
       Now it is stone, and stays.
```

*In a moment:* a change that ends by setting — something that was moving becomes fixed.

```
  BULWARK           ■ □ △ ━

       I stand before you.
       What strikes you strikes me first.
```

*In a moment:* a thing held in a bond — standing with someone, or in front of them.

```
  BOLT              □ ▦ ▲ ─

       Picks what burns.
       Nothing held — only the struck.
```

*In a moment:* something picked out and changed, with nothing kept. The poem names its own dark mark:
*nothing held*.

```
  COMPEL            □ ▦ △ ━

       The bond was yours.
       I take it, and it turns to me.
```

*In a moment:* a bond claimed by a line drawn — someone marks a tie as theirs.

```
  MEND              □ □ ▲ ━

       The tie was torn.
       I move along it — whole, or gone.
```

*In a moment:* a tie carried through change; whether it comes out whole is not in the chord.

### Three marks — the heavy workings

```
  SPHERE            ■ ▦ ▲ ─

       A shape, set in the air.
       It chooses, and it consumes —
       all within, undone.
```

*In a moment:* a bounded thing that sorts and transforms everything it holds.

```
  OATH              ■ ▦ △ ━

       Fixed words. A name. A line.
       What is sworn here cannot slip —
       the bond is made law.
```

*In a moment:* a bond fixed by a drawn line — a promise, with change held still.

```
  REFORGE           ■ □ ▲ ━

       Gather the broken back.
       Turn the shards toward what they were,
       and tie the thread again.
```

*In a moment:* something broken, carried back through change into its ties — repair.

```
  BANISH            □ ▦ ▲ ━

       Find the one to send.
       Loose it, dissolve it, and cut
       every tie it held.
```

*In a moment:* one thing singled out, changed, and loosened from every tie it had — an ending. The ties
weaken to nothing; nothing is pushed apart.

### Four marks — the whole hand

```
  GENESIS           ■ ▦ ▲ ━

       A thing, cut from nothing.
       It sorts, it turns, it binds —
       the whole hand open.
       What it makes, or unmakes,
       waits on the one who casts.
```

*In a moment:* every mark struck. The same four marks make or unmake; only the aim says which.

### No mark — the hand closed

```
  VOID              □ □ △ ─

       Nothing lit.
       The hand stays closed.
       This, too, is a move.
```

*In a moment:* nothing struck — the empty chord. It is the unmarked ground before the first cut, Part 1
§3's `∅` — not nothing; waiting — unless a repulsion is at work, which the codex cannot mark (§5). The
poem itself was written for another void, the codex says: not the ground but stillness, *change at zero,
the world intact*. A moment whose only content is a decision to hold back comes nearest to it: the chord
is still empty, and the decision goes in the aim. *This, too, is a move.* It is the only poem in which
nothing stops, divides, turns or reaches, and the only one that lands — that says what its moment comes
to mean (§6) — because in the stillness it was written for, the choice to hold back is the whole content.
The book says of it: *Some days the strongest thing in the book is the choice to cast none of it.*

## 5 · Moments, analyzed

Five moments, analyzed with the method of §3: one invented for the page, three from Part 1's poems, and
one from Part 1's own worked reading of a moment.

### An ordinary moment

This one is invented for the page. *A friend you fell out with a year ago sends one line — "I miss you."
You write back.*

1. **One beat, no meaning yet.** The beat is the reply. Whether it is good news waits for step 4.
2. **The four questions.** *Does a thing hold, come to stay, or slip away?* The two of you are in it, but
   no kept thing stays, arrives or slips away: dark, `□`. *Was a line drawn, or erased?* Nothing is
   sorted into this and not that: what the reply breaks is a silence, which is change, and whatever line
   the year held came down with the friend's message, before this beat. Your deciding to answer belongs
   to the aim, in step 4. Dark, `□`. *Did something change?* A year's silence breaks: lit, `▲`. *Is a tie
   at work?* One torn a year ago, moving again: lit, `━`.
3. **The chord.** `□ □ ▲ ━` is MEND: *The tie was torn. / I move along it — whole, or gone.* It floats
   (below): nothing in the chord holds the tie in place, and whether it comes out whole is not in it.
4. **The aim.** *Toward whole*: a bond deepening, and you drawn to it; your deciding to answer belongs
   here too. That is yours to say; the chord does not say it for you.
5. **The words, if you want them.** One line for the turn, one for the reach, none for a held thing or an
   edge, and stop before the meaning (§6).

Its mirror, every mark flipped (§8), is WARD, `■ ▦ △ ─`: a boundary drawn around something kept. As a
prompt, not a finding: the year before this beat may have been exactly that.

### Three of Part 1's poems

Part 1's poems were written from formulas. Translating a formula into a chord is nearly mechanical — `O`
lights the object or the cut (the formula does not say which; §12), `R+` the bond, `F` change, `R-`
nothing, and `V` becomes the aim — but asking the four questions of the poem itself can light more. The
three below show both, and what the codex sees and what it cannot.

**Exile.** *The window is lit. / Your hands on glass, not touching. / The light stays inside.* Part 1
writes it `O · R- · V+`: a distinct thing you want, and something between you and it that pushes back.
Translated with `O` on the object, that is ANCHOR, `■ □ △ ─`, with nothing for the glass. Asked of the
poem, the four questions give more. A thing holds: the lit window, *the light stays inside*. A line is
drawn: the glass passes the sight of the light and stops the hands, and in the codex a barrier is the
cut's work, not the bond's. Nothing changes. No tie holds; wanting the light is the aim, not a bond. The
chord is WARD, `■ ▦ △ ─` — *A line is drawn. / What may not pass, does not.* — and the aim is *drawn
toward it, from the wrong side of the line*. Read from inside, the same WARD is shelter; from outside, it
is exile. The chord is shared; which side you stand on is the reader's. What the codex cannot write is
Part 1's reading of the glass as a push between you and the light. A repulsion between two things has no
mark, so the codex can only draw it as a line.

**Haunt.** *What was there is still / warm at the edges — you reach / and the hand has gone.* Part 1
writes it `R+ · F · V+v`: a good bond quietly coming apart. Translated, that is MEND, `□ □ ▲ ━`. Asked of
the poem, it stays MEND if the warmth is the tie — at work, and going, with the hand already gone. Part
1's own commentary says of that image *something persists*, and a reader who lights the object for what
persists gets REFORGE, the chord of the house below; the two readings part at one mark (§11). Read as
MEND, it is the same chord as the reply above, under the opposite aim: *a good, leaving*. The codex's
poem for MEND ends *whole, or gone*; the aim is what chooses *gone*.

**Longing.** *Something is missing / that doesn't have a name yet. / The space knows its shape.* Part 1
writes it `V+ before O`: wanting, with nothing yet to want. Translated, it lights nothing — no object, no
cut, no change, no tie — and leaves an aim with nothing under it. Asked of the poem, a reader might light
the object for *the space*, or the cut for its outline — Part 1's own commentary, whose *cut* covers the
whole of `O`, as §6 explains, calls *the absence itself* the cut. The formula holds back both, because
both are `O` marks and the state has no object yet. Read off a lived moment, the empty chord is the
unmarked ground — unless a repulsion is at work (below) — and longing reads as what the codex's void
becomes when someone brings weight to it: ground that is not nothing, and is waiting.

### The ground line

Most of Part 1's rows write no object; a moment that has one is, in Part 1's word, a variant of its row
(Part 1 §2 and §3). The codex will not let the object go unwritten: an object you leave unmarked is not
in the chord, so a struck chord with neither the object nor the cut lit has nothing to stand on, and
*floats*: it sits on the floating side of what the codex calls the *ground line*. TRANSMUTE is change
before form, Part 1 §3's chaos; SENDING is a tie before a thing, its fusion; MEND is both at once. The
poems leave the missing thing unnamed on purpose. Floating is said of the chord, not of the moment: in
the reply above the friend is still there at the far end of the tie, but no kept thing stays or slips
away, so nothing in the chord holds the tie in place.

If the moment turns on a thing kept — one that holds, or one slipping away — light it, and the chord
changes. Part 1 §7's own reading of a moment shows how: *The house is sold. I still have the key on my
ring and I haven't taken it off.* The house is already gone, sold before the moment opens, so it does not
light the object; the key does, a kept thing still on the ring. The tie to the house is loosening, and
that loosening is the change in the moment — the tie's own, not motion around the key. The chord is
REFORGE, `■ □ ▲ ━`, under the aim *a good, leaving*: the poem's repair, run backward. Part 1 writes the
same moment `O · R+ · F · V+v` and calls it haunt, with nothing absent. A reader for whom the sale itself
is the edge of the moment lights the cut as well and gets GENESIS, and the two can point to the one mark
where they part (§11). Part 5 §12 left open whether an unwritten object counts as anchored — there enough
for a state to stand rather than float. The codex's answer is no: an object you leave unmarked does not
hold the chord up, and the chord floats.

### What the codex cannot see

The codex is blind in two places. It cannot write a repulsion between two things, and where a lived
yearning has a barrier, as Exile does, it can only draw it as a line. And it never sees weight at all:
its which way is the aim, and its size rides in the coat. So three different states share the empty
chord. For longing, a weight with nothing under it, the empty chord is the unmarked ground; for yearning
and revulsion, which Part 1 writes with no object, it is a blind spot, since a repulsion needs things to
push apart. Only the aim, and what the reader knows of a push the chord cannot show, tells them apart.

The same translation can be run on all of Part 1 §2's named states, and on longing from Part 1 §3.
Appendix C does it.

## 6 · Writing the words

Every chord carries a poem, and all sixteen follow one law.

```
  ■  object stops     the image that holds
  ▦  cut divides      the edge: this, not that
  ▲  change turns     something shifts
  ━  bond reaches     two held together
```

Each lit mark earns its part of the poem. A dark mark earns nothing, and *the leaving-out is the
accuracy.* A dark mark may be named as an absence or as ground — BOLT's *nothing held*, OATH's *cannot
slip*, ANCHOR's *the rest moves* — but it never gets an image of its own. In the codex's words, *one
dial, a single strike; four, a full breath.* The landing — what the moment comes to mean — stays out of
the poem, with the one exception named below: it belongs to the aim, and the aim is the reader's. *The
poem does not describe the spell. it does it.*

This is Part 1 §5's reading of the two-image haiku — the cut, the pairing, the turn, the landing — with
two changes. The cut is split in two, as the marks split `O`: an image that stops, and an edge that
divides. And the landing is taken out. It is stricter than Part 1 in one more way. Part 1 gives
permission to leave a missing seed out, and its own commentary still finds a cut, a relation and a turn
in every poem (Part 1 §5); the codex requires the leaving-out, for every dark mark, whether the chord
floats or not. Two poems handle the landing on purpose. GENESIS points at it without making it (*waits on
the one who casts*). The void is the only poem that lands, because in the stillness it was written for
(§4), the choice to hold back is the whole content.

To write your own: analyze a moment (§3), give each lit mark one line in its own motion — a thing that
holds, an edge, a turn, a reach — and stop before the meaning.

## 7 · What a glyph is

What §3 produces — a moment cut into four marks — is what the codex calls a glyph. It names the work
*glyphcraft*, *the craft of cutting a glyph*, and the word needs a definition here. The archive behind
the series uses it constantly; the earlier parts almost never do, and Part 4 uses it for a frame's sign
and leaves the meaning to this section.

In my own definition, a glyph is *a mark that only means something to a certain group — flexible enough
to carry meaning, structured enough to constrain the options.* *Mark*, there, is any written sign, and
the certain group is the readers who hold its key. An everyday case is a chord symbol on a song sheet,
Am7: to a guitarist a hand shape, to a pianist four notes under one hand, to anyone without the key three
characters. It is loose enough that two players need not voice it alike, and tight enough that both play
the same four notes.

A glyph does not contain its meaning. Each reader rebuilds the meaning from the sign, and the readings
are compatible, not identical (Part 1 §4); what survives all of them is the grain (Part 5). That is why a
glyph needs its key: symbols compress only when the reader can decompress them (Part 5 §7). The archive's
one-line claim for the idea is *one text, more than one kind of reader, no translation between them*:
each reads the same text straight, and none has to convert another's reading. The word comes from the
Greek for *carving*, and the codex's verb fits it: you *cut* a glyph.

As this part uses it, the word covers signs of four kinds:

```
  a seed           O    R+    F    V-       Part 1       one sign: one question about a moment
  a formula        R+ · F · V+v             Part 1       a state, written in seeds
  a frame's sign   ⟨O^⟩                     Part 4       a stance, named by the seed it holds still
  a chord          ■ □ ▲ ━                  this part    which of four marks were struck
```

Part 4 states the rule for frames, and it holds for all four: *names are aliases; the glyph is the
identity.* A chord is the plainest of the four, because its key is almost nothing — four positions, lit
or dark. An early draft of the codex, which calls the row of marks a *sigil* and uses *decode* for
looking a label up, put it this way: *the sigil is the structure, read straight off — not a label to
decode. unlike a lone letter (is "T" a Troll or a Tree?), a chord shows exactly which dials are turned.
so the sigil leads; the name follows.*

The archive also uses the word in a larger sense, for one grain carried through all four of Part 5 §7's
registers — math, poetry, diagram and toy — so that a reader fluent in any one of them can find it in the
others; this part calls that a *full glyph*. Written out with its poem (§6), a chord has the makings of a
small full glyph: the seeds beneath its marks for the math, its poem for the poetry, the analysis itself
for the toy. But its row of marks is notation rather than a diagram, and a chord is the shape of one
moment, not a grain. No chord has been carried through Part 5 §7's test, which the archive has applied
twice, both times self-scored. And a glyph, in the archive's phrase, *carries a whose*: two readers can
write the same moment as different glyphs, because they start from different places.

Of the four kinds above, the chord is the only one that never carries the weight. A seed or a formula can
write it as `V`; the chord leaves it to the reader.

## 8 · The mirror

Flip every mark of a chord — lit to dark, dark to lit — and you reach its mirror. In the codex's words,
*flip every mark, and the poem turns over too.* The sixteen fold into eight pairs. The book counts seven
and an exception, because it folds only the fifteen struck chords; the void, in its words, is *the spell
not cast*:

```
  ANCHOR     ⇄  BANISH       hold    · release
  DISCERN    ⇄  REFORGE      judge   · repair
  TRANSMUTE  ⇄  OATH         turn    · bind
  SENDING    ⇄  SPHERE       reach   · strike
  WARD       ⇄  MEND         bar     · tend
  PETRIFY    ⇄  COMPEL       fix     · sway
  BULWARK    ⇄  BOLT         guard   · throw

  the eighth:     GENESIS ⇄ VOID — the whole hand open, and the hand closed
```

Because the flip always changes the first mark, every pair sets a lit object against a dark one. Three of
the seven pairs between struck chords cross the ground line — WARD ⇄ MEND, TRANSMUTE ⇄ OATH, SENDING ⇄
SPHERE — each setting a chord with both the object and the cut lit against one with neither, which
floats: *the held thing against the floating one.* GENESIS ⇄ VOID has the same shape, but the void does
not float; it is the ground, or a blind spot where a repulsion is at work (§5).

No mirror falls outside the table. Every chord's opposite is already in it, so the mirror never points at
a missing tool, only at one you were not holding; the codex's own blind spots (§5) are another matter. An
early draft drew a conclusion about the reader's habits, not the codex's limits: *the blind spots, if
any, are not gaps — they are the dials you simply reach for less.* If the moments you analyze keep coming
out as the same few chords, their mirrors are one place to look for what you reach for less.

The mirror is the codex's own operation: the complement of a set. It is not the notation's own reversal,
which flips the direction on a single seed (`^` rising or forming, `v` falling or fading; Part 1 §2), and
the notation neither asks for the mirror nor forbids it. Whether the mirror of a moment tells you
anything about the moment is a question nobody has tested. As a prompt it is at least suggestive: a
moment that drew a boundary has, for its opposite, a tie carried through change, with no thing held and
no line drawn.

## 9 · Intensity, coat, and the reader

Three things change how a chord is lived without changing the chord.

**Intensity** is how hard a lit mark is driven. On change it is the notation's own `F`, a scale with no
top: the same TRANSMUTE is a mood turning and a life turning. Part 1's dread poem, `F[high] · V-^`, is
TRANSMUTE driven hard. On the other three marks, whether a seed carries a strength of its own is open —
Part 5 §4 and Part 4 §8 give every seed a value, but only as modelling choices — and the book simply
treats each as if it does. *Amplitude* — how loudly a moment rings, which Part 1 gives to the size of `V`
— is not intensity at all. It is the reader's, and the codex carries it in the coat.

**The coat** — the codex also calls it the *dressing* — is everything the chord wears: a texture (fire,
frost, salt, glass, tide…), a voice (a soft hush, an elegiac fade, a child's wonder, a villain's
relish…), and a form (a poem, a proverb, a ritual, a gesture). None of it touches the chord. The book's
own example holds the chord and its drive still and changes only the coat. BOLT in frost, in a soft hush:
*It looks, almost, like mercy.* The same BOLT in fire, in a villain's relish: *The same spell. Worn
cruel.* As the book puts it, *fire and frost are the same Bolt — only dressed apart.* The codex calls the
voice a *frame* and the form a *seed-form*; I use *voice* and *form* because both of its words already
mean something else in this series. The codex also counts intensity among the coats. This part keeps it
apart, because on change it is the notation's own `F`.

**The reader** brings the rest. Two people can live the same chord in different coats and come away with
different moments. Part 1 §4 writes this `P_a(X) ≠ P_b(X)`: two perceivers, two readings. The chord is
the part they can share, when they cut the same one. A reader can also misread. The book's own warning is
SPHERE — a chord with the bond dark — struck by one who *sees ties that are not there*: Wisdom's shadow
in Part 4 §7, the story that makes sense and therefore must be true. The book's verdict is *the dial
overdriven, the read gone wrong.* For anyone analyzing a moment it is the error to watch for: lighting
the bond because the story wants one.

## 10 · Where does the meaning live?

Near its end the codex puts one question to six seats at a table — *where does the magic live?* — and
keeps all six answers. They carry over to moments whole. Here they are as the codex gives them, with
three of the book's words changed: *magic* to *meaning*, *dials* to *marks*, *casting* to *reading*.

```
  formalist        in the structure. the chord is real; the four marks are really there.
  evolutionary     in us. minds built to read pattern as power — old, shared, not arbitrary.
  Kantian          in the judgment. mine, yet it expects that you will see the chord too.
  Humean           in the eye. no meaning in the marks — only what the reader carries in.
  constructivist   in the lineage. thirty years of text worlds taught the eye to read ● and ○.
  relational       in the meeting. neither page nor reader but the reading that needs them both.
```

The constructivist's *text worlds* are games played in text and symbols on a screen; its ● and ○ are the
book's earlier signs for lit and dark. *Six seats, one arc*, in the codex's phrase: `P_a(X) ≠ P_b(X)`,
different readers, different readings. In its words, *none of them is lying. the table is round on
purpose* — and the disagreement is the data, not the noise (Part 1 §7, Part 4 §10).

## 11 · What is claimed, at honest strength

- **The book is fiction, and the reading is yours.** It was written as a spellbook, and its own pages say
  *the spells make no claim beyond the page*. The same book tells you to decode your own moments with it.
  Both hold: analyzing a moment this way is a way of looking, and it claims nothing about the moment
  beyond the look.
- **Sixteen is arithmetic.** Two to the fourth. The table is complete and closed by construction; the
  open question is whether these are the right four marks.
- **The four marks are a re-cut of the seeds, not the seeds.** `O` is split in two, `R-` has no mark,
  direction moves from the seeds into the aim, and `V` is off the marks. A chord is coarser than a Part 1
  state; the aim recovers most of the rest, but not a repulsion (§5). The working rules in §3, the
  translation in Appendix C and the plain readings of each chord are mine.
- **The readings added in October are still proposals.** The seeds beneath the marks, the ground line,
  the note on the void, the mirror note, the note on intensity and the restated poem law were drafted in
  an October pass with an AI assistant and carried into the archive's main copy. The page still marks
  them as unadopted. The second mark is the one still open: an early draft marks it *not yet locked*, and
  its reading as the cut, drawn in October, is still a proposal.
- **The poems were audited, not tested.** In October each was read against its chord and none
  contradicted it; no poem was revised. That is a check of fit, not a test on readers.
- **The mirror is the codex's own, and so is intensity on every mark but change.** The notation neither
  licenses nor forbids the mirror. On change, intensity is the notation's own `F`; whether the other
  seeds carry a strength of their own is open.
- **An analysis is a reading, not a measurement.** Two readers may cut different chords from one moment,
  because they start from different places (§7). The marks then show them exactly where they part — one
  mark lit where the other is dark — a disagreement they can point at. The chord is a structure two
  readers can compare, not one they are guaranteed to share.
- **It reads moments, not people.** A table of sixteen built from four switches will remind some readers
  of a sixteen-type personality test. A chord is the shape of one moment, and nobody is a chord (Appendix
  B).
- **Nothing here has been tried on a reader who was not first handed the notation** (Part 4 §11, Part 5
  §11).

## 12 · Open questions

**A mark for repulsion.** The codex has none, and §5 shows what that costs: yearning and revulsion fall
into the empty chord beside longing, and a barrier like Exile's glass can only be drawn as a line. An
early draft gave the bond two poles, drawing together and driving apart; the October edition keeps one
and leaves the pulling-apart out. If the codex ever holds it again, the October survey that reconciled
the codex with the notation says it would be a new reading of an existing chord — BANISH is the candidate
— not a seventeenth chord.

**Which object.** When a Part 1 formula is translated, does its `O` light the object, the cut, or both?
The formulas do not say, and the chord insists on an answer (§2).

**The working rules.** The three rules in §3 are mine. Whether two readers who are given them cut the
same chord from the same moment has not been tried.

**Strength per mark.** Whether the object, the cut and the bond carry strengths of their own, or only
change does.

**The mirror of a moment.** Whether flipping a lived moment's chord says anything true about it.

**Chords that compose.** Two chords can be combined — light every mark either one lights — but nothing
says what happens when one chord acts on what another left, where order would matter. Until something
does, the count of sixteen says nothing about the world (Part 1 §8).

---

## 13 · Closing

A chord is the bones of a moment: which of four things were struck. The coat is how it was lived, and the
aim is what it was for. The codex keeps the three apart — *the chord is the spell. the drama is the
dressing.* — so that the part two people can share is never mistaken for the part only one of them can
bring. In the book's own words: *the marks are mine to cut; the meaning is yours to mean.*

*A lens, not a law.*

## Appendix B · Where the ideas come from

Part 1's Appendix B carries the notation's sources, and Part 5's re-checks the eigenform's and adds the
grain's. The codex needs only a little more, in the same tiers, with one added at the end for a
resemblance to rule out. Where only a record or a summary could be reached, the entry says so. None of
the analogies below is a source of the codex. The archive it comes from cites none of them, and names
only one, the boundary object.

### Mathematics (imported)

- **B. A. Davey & H. A. Priestley, *Introduction to Lattices and Order*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University
  Press (2002),** chapter 4. The sixteen chords are the subsets of a four-element set — its power set —
  and ordered by inclusion they form a Boolean lattice. Its diagram is the four-dimensional cube: sixteen
  corners, each edge joining two chords one mark apart, VOID and GENESIS at opposite corners. Counted by
  lit marks the corners fall 1 · 4 · 6 · 4 · 1, row 4 of Pascal's triangle, counting the single 1 at the
  top as row 0. The mirror is the lattice complement; it sends a chord with *k* lit marks to one with 4 −
  *k*, which is why the row reads the same both ways. The chapter list was checked, not the text; the
  facts used are standard.

### Analogy (shape only)

- **Geomancy.** The Arabic *ʿilm al-raml*, "the science of the sand", and its Latin, West African and
  Malagasy relatives build sixteen figures from four lines of one or two points. An operation that modern
  overviews call *inversion* turns every line to its opposite — the codex's mirror exactly. It is a
  divinatory tradition; the codex divines nothing. Read in overviews; the scholarly sources they cite —
  Stephen Skinner, *Terrestrial Astrology: Divination by Geomancy* (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul,
  1980), and Marcia Ascher, "Malagasy Sikidy: A Case in Ethnomathematics," *Historia Mathematica* 24(4):
  376–395 (1997) — were not reached.
- **The I Ching.** Figures of three or six broken or unbroken lines: eight trigrams, sixty-four
  hexagrams. Its complementary hexagram changes every line, the same operation as the mirror, on a
  different number of lines. Read in overviews.
- **G. W. Leibniz, "Explication de l'arithmétique binaire," *Mémoires de l'Académie royale des sciences*,
  année 1703, pp. 85–89.** Read, in a scan of the original pages. Leibniz reads the Chinese line figures
  as binary numbers, a whole line for one and a broken line for zero (p. 88).
- **Allen Forte, *The Structure of Atonal Music*, Yale University Press (1973).** The complement of a
  pitch-class set is every pitch class it leaves out. The word *chord* is shared; the universe is twelve
  pitch classes, not four marks, and nothing else carries over. Table of contents only.
- **Susan Leigh Star & James R. Griesemer, "Institutional Ecology, 'Translations' and Boundary Objects:
  Amateurs and Professionals in Berkeley's Museum of Vertebrate Zoology, 1907–39," *Social Studies of
  Science* 19(3): 387–420 (1989).** A boundary object is flexible enough to serve each group that uses it
  and robust enough to keep a shared identity across all of them (p. 393) — close to the definition of a
  glyph in §7. Star and Griesemer describe such objects as a means of translating between social worlds,
  where the archive's glyph claims to need no translation between its readers. Read at p. 393, in a scan
  of the published pages. The archive draws the comparison itself, without citing a source.

### Not this

- **The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator.** Four either-or dichotomies make its sixteen personality types, and
  its reliability and validity are widely criticized. The codex sorts moments, not people, and measures
  nothing. Read in overviews.

## Appendix C · Part 1's states as chords

§5's translation, run on all of Part 1 §2's named states and on longing from Part 1 §3: `O` lights the
object or the cut, `R+` lights the bond, `F` lights change, `R-` lights nothing, and `V` with its
direction becomes the aim. The table is my translation, not the archive's.

```
  state               formula          chord        marks       the aim supplies
  love                R+ · V+          SENDING      □ □ △ ━     drawn to it
  contempt · trapped  R+ · V-          SENDING      □ □ △ ━     pushed from it
  growth              F · V+^          TRANSMUTE    □ □ ▲ ─     a good, rising
  grief               F · V+v          TRANSMUTE    □ □ ▲ ─     a good, leaving
  dread               F · V-^          TRANSMUTE    □ □ ▲ ─     a bad, growing
  recovery            F · V-v          TRANSMUTE    □ □ ▲ ─     a bad, receding
  flow · siege        F · V± holding   TRANSMUTE    □ □ ▲ ─     a good · a bad, holding
  precious · taboo    O · V±           ANCHOR       ■ □ △ ─     drawn · pushed
  haunt               R+ · F · V+v     MEND         □ □ ▲ ━     a good, leaving
  reconciliation      R+ · F · V-v     MEND         □ □ ▲ ━     a bad, receding
  yearning            R- · V+          VOID         □ □ △ ─     drawn, across a repulsion with no mark
  revulsion           R- · V-          VOID         □ □ △ ─     pushed, likewise
  longing             V before O       VOID         □ □ △ ─     a weight with nothing under it
```

*If the precious or taboo thing is being cut rather than held, its chord is DISCERN. Part 1's complete
state, `O · R · F · V` — a whole moment — is GENESIS when its object is both held and cut and its bond
holds.*

**Six of Part 1's formulas share one chord.** Growth, grief, dread, recovery, flow and siege, as Part 1
writes them, are all TRANSMUTE. The chord is the bones; the aim and its direction tell them apart. This
is Part 1's rule that good and bad live only in `V`, seen from the chord's side: take `V` out, and six
formulas share their bones. A lived grief usually has a tie in it as well and lights the bond; Haunt is
grief's chord with that tie written in.
